title: Venture Capital, Explained
---
I didn't know much about venture capital before joining AngelList. But after building products for VCs, watching funds get raised on our platform, and seeing startups go through their fundraising processes, I've learned a lot more about how the industry works.

This is my guide to the basics of venture capital
### Where the money comes from

To start a venture fund, you need two things: startups to invest in, and money to invest with.

The money comes from Limited Partners (LPs). LPs can be wealthy individuals or family offices, institutions like pension funds or university endowments. A pension fund managing retirement savings for teachers, for example, might allocate 5-10% of its portfolio to venture capital as part of a broader investment strategy. They're looking for high returns, but they also know venture is risky and illiquid—their money will be locked up for years.

LPs don't pick startups themselves. They commit capital and trust someone else to manage it. That person is the General Partner (GP). The GP runs the fund, finds startups, decides where to invest, and manages the portfolio over time. GPs are often former founders, operators, or people who have spent years in the startup ecosystem.

LPs provide the money. GPs decide how it gets invested. This separation is important because LPs often don't have the time or expertise to evaluate hundreds of startups, and GPs can focus entirely on finding the best opportunities.

### How fund managers make money

Once a fund is raised, the GP needs to cover operating costs and eventually make money if the fund performs well. Fund managers make money in two ways: management fees and carry.

Management fees are typically around 2% per year of the total fund size. If a fund raises \$100M, that's \$2M per year to cover operations—paying employees, lawyers, accountants, travel, and general expenses. This fee is charged regardless of whether the fund makes money, which gives GPs a baseline income to run the fund.

Carry (short for "carried interest") is where the real upside is. After the fund returns LPs their original investment, any remaining profits are split, usually 80% to LPs and 20% to GPs. If a \$100M fund returns \$300M, LPs get their \$100M back first, then the remaining \$200M is split: \$160M to LPs, \$40M to GPs.

Most venture funds have a lifecycle of about 10 years. Carry is earned only if the fund performs well, which aligns incentives between LPs and GPs. If the fund doesn't return at least the original capital, GPs don't see carry. This structure pushes GPs to focus on finding companies that can generate massive returns.

### Types of venture funds

There are a few common fund structures, each solving slightly different problems.

**Traditional venture funds** follow the classic model: the GP raises a fixed amount of capital (say, \$50M or \$500M), charges 2% management fees, takes 20% carry, and operates over a 10-year lifecycle. The GP invests that capital over the first 3-5 years, then spends the remaining years managing the portfolio and helping companies grow until they exit. Once the fund is raised, the GP can't easily raise more capital—they have to wait until the next fund.

**Rolling funds** were pioneered at AngelList. Instead of raising one large fund upfront, GPs raise capital on a recurring basis, often quarterly. Every quarter, LPs commit a certain amount, and the GP invests it into startups. This gives GPs a continuous source of capital and lets them invest more consistently over time. It also lowers the barrier to entry for new GPs—raising \$5M on a quarterly commitment can sometimes be easier than raising a \$50M fund upfront.

**SPVs (Special Purpose Vehicles)** are funds created for a single company. If a GP finds a particularly promising startup, they might create an SPV to let multiple investors pool money specifically for that deal. Investors know exactly which startup their money is going into, which is appealing for people who want more control over where their capital goes.

Each structure has trade-offs. Traditional funds give GPs a large pool of capital to deploy strategically. Rolling funds offer flexibility but require ongoing fundraising. SPVs are narrow but transparent. The structure a GP chooses depends on their strategy, their relationship with LPs, and how they want to deploy capital.

### How startups are funded

Traditional small businesses are often funded with bank loans. A restaurant borrows \$200K, uses it to build out the space and buy equipment, and pays it back over time with interest as revenue comes in. This works when cash flow is predictable.

Startups are different. Early-stage startups often have no revenue and no clear timeline for making money. A software company might spend two years building a product before seeing its first dollar of revenue. Because of that, loans don't work—there's nothing reliable to repay them with, and banks aren't willing to take that risk.

Instead, startups raise equity. Investors give the company money in exchange for ownership. If the company succeeds, investors make money by selling their shares later. If it fails, they lose everything. This is fundamentally different from a loan—there's no obligation to pay it back, but investors only make money if the company grows significantly.

In the earliest stages, startups often use instruments like SAFEs (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) or convertible notes. These are not quite equity yet—they're promises that convert into equity later.

Here's how a SAFE works: an investor gives a startup \$500K. Instead of receiving shares immediately, they get a contract that says "when this company raises a priced round, I'll convert this \$500K into shares at a discount or valuation cap." This delays the valuation negotiation until the company is further along and has more data to base a valuation on.

A priced round is when investors agree on what the company is worth today. If a company raises \$5M at a \$20M pre-money valuation, that means investors are valuing the company at \$20M before the new money goes in. After the \$5M is invested, the company is worth \$25M (post-money), and investors own 20% (\$5M / \$25M). At this point, SAFEs and convertible notes convert into actual equity, usually with a discount or cap to reward early investors for taking more risk.

These instruments exist because startup outcomes are so uncertain. Investors accept the risk of losing everything in exchange for the possibility of very large returns. But startups don't raise all their money at once—they raise it in stages.

### What Series A, B, C actually mean

Startups raise money in rounds because their needs and risks change as they grow. Raising too much money too early can lead to waste. Raising too little can mean running out of cash before proving the idea works. Rounds let startups raise what they need at each stage.

**Seed** is the earliest stage. The company is often just an idea, a prototype, and a pitch deck. The founders might be a small team—two or three people—trying to build the first version of their product. Seed rounds are typically \$500K to \$3M and are used to get to product-market fit: build something, get it in front of users, and figure out if people actually want it. Investors at this stage are betting almost entirely on the team, because there's not much else to evaluate.

**Series A** usually means the company has found some product-market fit. They've proven people want what they're building—maybe they have thousands of users, early revenue, or strong engagement metrics. Now they're raising capital to scale. This might mean hiring engineers, building out sales and marketing, or expanding to new markets. Series A rounds are typically \$5M to \$15M. The valuation is usually higher than at Seed because the company has reduced some of the early risk.

**Series B and beyond** means the company is growing and needs capital to expand faster. Maybe they're entering new geographies, launching new product lines, or scaling operations to handle increasing demand. At this stage, the company has proven the business model works—now it's about execution and speed.

Each round comes with higher expectations. Seed investors expect the company to find product-market fit. Series A investors expect revenue growth and a clear path to profitability. Series B investors expect the company to dominate a market or scale quickly. The later the round, the more the company has to prove, but also the more capital it can raise.

But raising money isn't free. Every time a startup raises a round, something important happens: dilution.

### Dilution and control

When a startup raises money, it creates new shares. This dilutes everyone's ownership percentage.

Here's a simplified example: a founder starts a company and owns 100% of 1 million shares. They raise a Seed round, and investors buy 250,000 newly created shares for \$1M. Now there are 1.25 million shares total, and the founder owns 80% (1M / 1.25M). Later, the company raises a Series A, creating another 500,000 shares. Now there are 1.75 million shares total, and the founder owns about 57% (1M / 1.75M).

The company is worth more, but the founder owns less of it. This is the trade-off: give up ownership to grow faster. If the company becomes worth \$100M and the founder owns 50%, that's \$50M. If they had kept 100% but the company only grew to \$10M because it didn't have capital to scale, they'd be worse off.

But it's not just about ownership. When investors put in money, they often take a board seat. The board makes major decisions about the company's direction—hiring and firing executives, approving budgets, deciding whether to raise more capital, approving acquisitions or sale offers.

Early on, the board might be just the founder and one or two investors. But as the company raises Series B, C, and beyond, the board grows. More investors, more voices, more people with input on major decisions. This is very different from a traditional small business, where the owner controls everything.

Founders have to balance growth with control. Raising more capital means growing faster, but it also means bringing in external advisers and giving up decision-making power. Some founders raise only what they need and try to keep control as long as possible. Others raise aggressively and accept that control will be shared. It's a strategic choice, and there's no single right answer.

Understanding dilution and control helps explain why founders care so much about who they raise money from. It's not just about getting capital—it's about who will have a say in the company's future.

### How venture funds choose startups

In public markets, investors have data. They can look at Apple's revenue, profit margins, quarterly earnings, debt levels, and historical performance. They can model future cash flows and decide whether the stock is undervalued.

In early-stage startups, almost none of that data exists. A Seed-stage company might have no revenue, no users, and no finished product. A Series A company might have revenue but no profits and no clear path to profitability yet. The data that public market investors rely on just isn't there.

Because of that, venture investing is heavily founder-driven. Investors spend a lot of time evaluating the team. Do the founders have relevant experience? Have they built something before? Do they understand the problem deeply? Can they recruit great people? How do they think about trade-offs? How do they handle setbacks?

In technical fields like AI or biotech, investors also evaluate the founders' ability to execute on hard problems. A founder working on a new AI model needs to understand the research landscape, have a sense of where the field is going, and be able to attract top researchers. A founder building a biotech company needs to navigate FDA approvals, clinical trials, and complex regulatory environments.

There's still analysis involved—investors look at market size, competitive dynamics, and whether the problem is worth solving. But ultimately, they're making a bet on whether this team can build something valuable in a space that's still forming. It's much more about belief in people and ideas than hard numbers.

This is also why venture investors talk to a lot of founders. A VC might take 100 meetings to make one investment. They're trying to find the rare founder who has a compelling vision, deep expertise, and the ability to execute. Most pitches don't lead anywhere, but the few that do can define a fund.
### How startups choose VCs

In a strong fundraising environment where capital is more available, the dynamic flips. Hot startups that are growing quickly often have multiple investors competing to fund them. In those cases, startups get to choose who they raise from.

When that happens, VCs have to offer more than just money. A lot of people can write a check. What differentiates one VC from another is what else they bring.

Some VCs have deep talent networks or partner with recruiting firms, giving startups easier access to top-tier engineers, designers, or executives. Others have large social media followings or popular podcasts, which can help startups with distribution and visibility. Some have deep industry connections—maybe a partner spent years operating in fintech or healthcare and can introduce startups to their first customers or key partnerships in that space.

This is why founders care so much about who they raise from. It's not just about the terms or the valuation. It's about who will help them when things get hard, who has the relationships they need, and who understands their market well enough to be useful.
### The power law

The power law explains everything about venture returns.

In a typical venture fund, most investments fail or return very little. Out of 100 companies, maybe 50 will go to zero. Another 30 might return 1x or less—the fund gets its money back but doesn't make a profit. Maybe 15 will return 2-5x—solid, but not enough to move the needle on a large fund.

Then there are 2 or 3 companies that return 50x, 100x, or even 1000x the original investment. These are the outliers, and they generate the majority of the fund's returns. A single company can return an entire fund. If a VC invests \$1M in a company at a \$10M valuation and it grows to \$10B, that's a 1000x return—\$1B back from a single investment.

This is very different from public markets, where returns are more evenly distributed. If you invest in 100 public companies, you might expect most to return somewhere between -20% and +50%, with a few outliers. In venture, you expect most to return zero, and all your gains to come from a tiny number of winners.

This is why venture investors focus so much on potential scale. A company that becomes moderately successful—say, worth \$50M or \$100M—doesn't matter much to a large fund. If a VC puts \$5M into a company and it exits at \$50M, that's a 10x return, which sounds great. But if the fund is \$200M, that \$50M return only moves the needle by 2.5%. The fund needs multiple 50x or 100x outcomes to generate strong overall returns.

This also explains why VCs are willing to take big risks on unproven ideas. If most investments fail anyway, the strategy isn't to minimize failures—it's to maximize the chance of finding an outlier. A VC would rather invest in 10 high-risk, high-reward companies than 10 safe, moderate-return companies, because the upside in venture comes entirely from the extreme winners.

But for a venture fund to realize those returns, there has to be a way for investors to turn their ownership into cash. That happens through exits.

### How funds make returns

Once a fund invests in a startup, it eventually needs a way to turn that investment back into cash. Until that happens, the value is just on paper. A VC might own shares in a company worth \$1B, but they can't pay LPs with shares—they need actual money.

This usually happens through an exit. There are two common types:

**Acquisition:** another company buys the startup and purchases all shares, including those owned by investors. This is the most common exit. A large tech company might acquire a smaller startup for its technology, team, or product. For example, Google acquired DeepMind for around \$500M in 2014. Investors and founders get paid out, usually in cash or stock in the acquiring company.

**IPO (Initial Public Offering):** the company goes public, and shares can be sold on the public market. This is rarer and usually happens only for larger, more mature companies. When a company IPOs, it lists its shares on a stock exchange, and anyone can buy them. Early investors can sell their shares over time, though there are usually lock-up periods that prevent them from selling immediately.

Venture is a long-term and illiquid asset class. Unlike public stocks, which you can sell at any time, venture investments are locked up for years. It's common for a VC to invest in a company in 2020 and not see an exit until 2028 or 2030. During that time, the value is mostly on paper, and there's no guarantee the company will succeed.

Sometimes liquidity happens earlier through secondary sales (selling shares to another investor) or company buybacks (the company buys back shares from early investors). But these are less common and usually require company approval. The company might allow secondary sales if an early employee wants to cash out, but investors can't just sell whenever they want.

This long time horizon is why LPs commit capital for 10+ years. They know they won't see returns for a long time, and they're okay with that because the potential upside is high. But it also means venture isn't for everyone—if you need access to your money in the next few years, venture is the wrong asset class.

So why do startups go through this process? Why raise money, dilute ownership, and take on the pressure to grow into a billion-dollar company?

### Why startups raise venture capital

Startups raise venture capital to grow faster than they could through revenue alone.

Some businesses—especially deep tech companies—require large upfront investment before they can generate income. A biotech company might spend \$50M on research and clinical trials before it can sell a single product. An AI company might need \$20M to hire researchers and buy compute to train models. Without venture capital, these companies couldn't exist.

Other startups could grow organically, but they'd lose to competitors who are growing faster. If you're building a consumer app and a competitor raises \$20M to spend on marketing and product development while you're bootstrapping, they'll likely win the market. In competitive spaces, raising capital is often necessary just to keep up.

Venture capital gives startups the resources to move quickly—hire faster, build more features, expand to new markets, and out-execute competitors. The trade-off is dilution, loss of control, and pressure to grow into the kind of company that can return the fund. If you raise \$10M from a VC, they're expecting you to build a company worth hundreds of millions or more. Anything less is a disappointment.

Not every company should raise venture capital. If you're building a profitable business that can grow steadily without outside capital, you might be better off bootstrapping. You'll own more of the company, control your own decisions, and not be pressured to chase hypergrowth.

But for companies trying to become worth billions—whether that's because they're in a capital-intensive industry or because they're in a winner-take-all market—venture capital is often the only way to get there fast enough.
