title: The Career Game
---
**_An ongoing list of things I've learned working in the "real world"_**

### The hidden job market 

As an engineer, I believed hiring was purely meritocratic—your skills determined everything, and networking was barely relevant beyond maybe getting a referral. I was wrong. Most good opportunities, especially at startups, happen through what I'd call a "gray market" for talent. Companies don't always post formal roles; they're hiring opportunistically, and if they meet someone talented, they'll create a position.

I got my job at AngelList this way - I met someone at a networking event, showed genuine interest in their work, and asked good questions. They connected me with recruiting and a role materialized. Great companies are always looking for talent, and if you make a good impression, opportunities follow naturally. Find companies you admire, talk to people there, and express how your experiences and interests align with what the company does. Most importantly, be genuinely curious about their work, don't just ask for a referral. 

I know its tough out there for new grads right now, but never appear appear desperate when looking for a job. If you make it seem that no other companies want you, why would this company take the chance? Instead of saying "it's a tough job market" or "I can't find anything." frame it as if you're being selective, not struggling. The difference in how people perceive you is enormous.

### Meet as many people as possible when you're new

The most important thing at any company is the people. Your long-term career is shaped by who you work with more than anything else. I've seen firsthand people who joined from a different company bring talent from their old company into something new, and when people start their own venture, the first hires are usually those that they have worked with before. It is more than likely that you're future opportunity will come from someone you already work with.  The time when building these relationships is easy is when you're new. Being the new person is the least awkward time to introduce yourself. You can reach out to anyone—"I saw you touched this part of the codebase," "I enjoyed your talk at all-hands"—and ask for a chat. People expect it from someone who just joined. So you should meet as many people as possible early on, since this leverage expires quickly. I didn't use it well enough at AngelList, and it's something I'd do differently next time I start somewhere.

### Visibility mattes more than hard work

Hard work and merit alone aren't enough to advance your career. You need to actually get recognition for what you've done, and a lot of the time you'll have to seek that recognition yourself. I was lucky that my manager asked me to present at the company-wide all-hands pretty early into my role at AngelList. That was huge for people knowing who I was and for opportunities coming my way later. When I started writing publicly about some of the work I was doing, I got a ton of outreach from people who were curious about how I'd built certain things, and even got me invites to present my work at AI events in New York.  Both kinds of visibility—publicly (to build a personal brand) and within the company (to be staffed on important projects)—are incredibly important for people to know who you are and understand what you're actually doing. 

You can get this visibility in one of two ways. Either by joining a team that is working on the highest-priority initiatives the company has outlined, or by figuring out how to communicate why what you're already working on matters. This isn't something that just happens from working hard. You have to actively seek recognition and look for opportunities to make your work more widely known.

### Don't over-index on compensation early in your career. 

I had a decision between two companies after graduating. One was a large public tech company offering a signing bonus and public shares that would have put my total compensation 15-20% higher than the other option. The higher-paying role meant traditional backend engineering work at an established company. The other was a faster-growing startup offering an AI engineering role I actually wanted. Even accounting for potential equity, the big tech company was the better financial outcome. 

When deciding between the two I tired to look at my choices from a long-term horizon (which is often really difficult to do). The main question I was trying to answer was, which of the two opportunities would be bring me a step closer to what my ideal job would look like? Looking back, joining AngelList was clearly the right choice. I got to build AI products from scratch, and was able to get a lot of visibility on my work too. I will of course never know what my career could have looked like had I joined the public company, but I do think that in the grand scheme of things everything else—salary, benefits, perks—balances out over time, so you might as well pick something that you enjoy more.

To be clear, I'm not suggesting that the startup opportunity is always better; sometimes the higher pay is genuinely the right outcome. But think carefully about how much the actual difference matters now and what your ideal career looks like. 

### Always be recruiting

Pretty soon in your career (and especially during hot job markets) you'll have recruiters or companies reach out to you for roles instead of the other way around. When that does happen, take the introductory call. If you're interested in the company, go through the interview process even if you're not ready to leave your current job. Companies that are growing quickly will always have a need for talented people, so if it's not the right time now, they'll likely still want you later when it is. Going through interviews keeps your skills sharp and lets you know what the market is willing to compensate you. It can be tiring to do this consistently, but it builds your network in ways that compound. In the process, always communicate your thoughts on staying/joining candidly so that you don't burn any bridges and don't set any false expectations.

###  Have friends at work

Make friends at work for two reasons. First, if you're excited to see the people you work with and can speak to them about things outside of work, it'll make your day-to-day life far more enjoyable. Second, these are the people who will advocate for you. Most companies have some system of recognition—whether it's a Slack channel for shoutouts, kudos software, or something similar. Your friends are the ones who will put in the extra effort to give you credit when you deserve it. I initially didn't think I needed to invest in relationships at work, I didn't feel the need to go to post-work events or make an effort to make plans with the people I worked with because I already had an established friend group in New York. Over time, I got closer to the people I worked with and now people I met at AngelList are good friends of mine that I stay in touch with even after leaving the company. Looking back I would've probably built those relationships sooner.

### Companies are more flexible than they appear, but only after you prove yourself. 

Job titles and responsibilities feel fixed when you start. They're not, especially at smaller companies where you can reshape your role toward what you actually want to do. But nobody will ask you what you want and hand it to you. I've watched people transition into completely different roles, but it happened because they proved they could do the work first. They showed they cared about something, put in effort to build the skills, and demonstrated they were capable. Only then did the people already doing that work become willing to mentor them and support the transition. The sequence matters: you do the work first, earn recognition second, and gain flexibility third. Companies are run by people, and people will let you redefine your role if you convince them you should. But you have to earn that flexibility by proving you deserve it.
